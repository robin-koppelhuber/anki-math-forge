what this stage decides

Two questions, and only two. Is this worth a card at all, and roughly what would the card be about.

Everything on screen serves those two: the crop, the sentence you marked, what you wrote beside it, the neighbours. You do not have to be able to transcribe a unit to triage it. A marked passage carries its own sentence. A boxed figure carries none and is still obviously worth a card, or obviously not.

If the answer needs more than yes, and it is about X, write a brief. {{ key('queue-with-brief') }} queues and records one in the same keystroke.

Depth belongs to the card stage, where /extract-cards and /augment have the page, the pages either side and the source's conventions, and iterate until the card can be approved. Here it costs throughput and buys nothing.

the whole machine

the unit

where it is
the section, the page, and the number the source printed if it numbers things. The id is built from whatever the source names it by: an equation number in a book that has them, a Zotero annotation key in a document you marked up. Neither is a position, so marking up more of a document renumbers nothing.
the picture
rendered from the PDF on demand, with a margin of surrounding page and the unit's own box drawn on it. How much margin is crop_context, and a source may set its own. The box is what this unit claims to be. If it holds two numbered equations, or the equation runs on outside it, segmentation got it wrong: annotate the unit rather than card it. {{ key('pdf-view') }} cycles the crop, the whole page, and the whole document scrolling.
the colours on it
what a reader marked, painted back on in the colours they used. The mark this unit came from is at full strength and everything marked nearby is faded, so a page with six highlights on it still says which one the card is about. A unit that came from a mark is cut to the full width of the page. A mark's box is the union of the lines a sentence spanned, so its left and right edges are only wherever that sentence started and stopped mid-column.
the marks underneath
what each one says: the sentence it covers, and anything written beside it. Whether a marked claim is worth a card on its own is mostly a question about its neighbours, and these are them. One is marked its own unit and you will meet it again; the rest are context for this decision. Two filters cut across the list: a group heading folds a kind away, and the swatches above them switch a colour off. Both are yours alone and change nothing on disk. Neither is the rail's "marked as" filter, which decides which units you meet at all, and narrowing the queue to green claims while hiding the purple terms beside them would remove exactly the context this decision needs.
the maths beside it
the transcription, rendered. Treat it as a hint. Where it disagrees with the picture, the picture wins. transcribed means something read the crop; not transcribed means run /transcribe, or read the crop yourself.
the grey text underneath
the sentence the book printed just above the equation, to introduce it. Context for deciding. It is not a transcription.

what each key does

{{ key('queue') }} queue
This becomes a card. /extract-cards reads everything queued and writes a stub for it. Nothing reaches Anki until you approve the card afterwards, in the review view.
{{ key('queue-with-brief') }} queue, and say what it is about
The same, plus the @claude brief, in one keystroke. This is the second half of what this stage decides. One sentence is enough: two cards, one per convention, this is about why the bound is tight, not the bound, merge with the one above. Symmetric with {{ key('skip') }}/{{ key('skip-with-reason') }}: the bare key does not stop to ask, and the shifted one records the sentence that makes the decision useful a month later.
{{ key('skip') }} skip
Never offer this again. Sticky, so it survives re-extraction.
{{ key('skip-with-reason') }} skip, with a reason
The same, but asks for one word first. Plain {{ key('skip') }} does not, because a skip is the commonest key in triage and stopping to type turns one keystroke into a dialogue.
{{ key('back-to-new') }} back to new
Undo a decision: the unit returns to the untriaged pool. This is the only way back from a skip.
{{ key('accept-suggestion') }} accept · {{ key('dismiss-suggestion') }} dismiss
Act on a suggestion, or throw it away. Suggestions come from forge classify or /classify, and both only ever propose. Pressing {{ key('queue') }} or {{ key('skip') }} yourself also clears the suggestion, since you overruled it.
{{ key('note-claude') }} / {{ key('note-me') }} the brief, and what you parked
{{ key('note-claude') }} writes the @claude brief: the instruction /extract-cards reads before it writes anything. "Two cards, one per layout convention", "the transcription is wrong, read the crop", "merge with the one above". It is the only instruction that pass gets, which is why it sits open in the panel rather than behind a fold. {{ key('note-me') }} parks an @me decision for yourself instead. Both show up in forge todo.
answering an @me
yes, no, or your own words. The answer is written back with the question (same as 2.4? — no) and unaddressed, so it reads as a record rather than new work. The × deletes without recording anything, which is right when the note was a reminder rather than a question.
{{ key('context-size') }} context size
How much of the document whoever writes this card is handed. The numbers are pages either side: 3 hands over seven pages, this one and three each way. Every size is on the chip, so click one or press {{ key('context-size') }} to walk them. Sizes rather than a number box, because the decision is "a bit more" or "all of it" and not a measurement. Triage is the moment you can tell, when the theorem is here and its hypotheses are two pages back and no per-source default knows that. The badge is always on screen and says whose number it is, source until this unit overrides it.
{{ key('web-lookups') }} web lookups
Whether whoever writes this card may look things up on the web. Off everywhere unless you grant it. A card is supposed to say what this source says, hypotheses and notation included, and the web is full of cleaner statements of the general theorem. Substitute one of those for the printed one and you get what looks like a better card, until the thing you are studying turns on the condition the paper had and the internet did not. Grant it here, on a unit that cites a result the source never states. Three states, not two: allowed, no, and neither, which falls back to the source and then the repo. Click the one already in force to clear the override. A card written from a granted unit inherits the grant, and forge context says which of the three answered.
{{ key('pdf-view') }} crop / page / document
Three ways to look at the same geometry. page is the whole page with the unit's own box drawn on it, which is the only way to see how segmentation failed: a split equation shows its missing lines outside the box, and two merged into one show two numbers inside it. document is every page, scrolling, landing on this one, for when the question is not about the region at all but about what the passage is saying three paragraphs up. Three named buttons in the corner of the picture do the same in one click. Clicking the picture itself does nothing, deliberately: it used to cycle the view, so leaning in to read a highlight jumped you to the whole document.
{{ key('undo') }} undo
Undo the last decision, however it was made: a state change, an accepted suggestion, a dismissed one. It restores what was actually there before, including the reason and any suggestion the action cleared, so a mis-pressed key costs one keystroke. Repeat to walk further back.
{{ key('next') }} / {{ key('prev') }}
Next / previous. Queuing and skipping move you on to the next unit still awaiting a decision, but the one you just acted on stays in the list, so {{ key('prev') }} always gets you back to it.
{{ key('filters') }} filters · {{ key('guide') }} this guide
Both panels toggle. The guide cycles three sizes: counts, then everything, then hidden. Drag either edge to resize it. The left rail is what acts; this one only tells you what you are looking at.
{{ key('sources') }} sources

what this stage decides

Whether the content is right. The unit stage already settled that this is worth a card and roughly what about, and nothing here revisits that. What is open is the wording, the hypotheses, the grading and the order: everything that has to be true before it reaches Anki.

This is where depth belongs. /augment has the page, the pages either side and the source's conventions, and iterates until check passes. A card can be revised any number of times while it is a draft. Approving is the only step that feels irreversible, and it is yours alone.

what each key does

{{ key('approve') }} approve
This card is right. Stamps content_hash, and sync will push it to Anki. Editing it afterwards un-approves it: the hash stops matching and it returns here.
{{ key('back-to-draft') }} back to draft
Un-approve a card you have changed your mind about. Editing one does this as a side effect, since content_hash stops matching, but changing your mind is not an edit and a token change in the editor would be a worse way to say so. Clears the hash, so nothing syncs it.
{{ key('reject') }} reject
This card is wrong and is not worth fixing. It stays on disk and nothing syncs it.
{{ key('editor') }} open in $EDITOR
Fix it yourself. The app re-reads from disk, and refuses to write over a file that changed underneath it.
{{ key('undo') }} undo
Undo the last approve or reject. Approving stamps content_hash and rejecting drops it, so this restores the hash too: the card goes back to the state it was actually in, not merely to the same status. The card you just acted on stays in the list, so {{ key('prev') }} reaches it.
{{ key('note-claude') }} / {{ key('note-me') }} annotate
{{ key('note-claude') }} leaves an @claude note, a request for Claude. {{ key('note-me') }} parks an @me decision instead. Sync refuses any card with an open annotation of either kind, whatever its status, so the note alone holds the card back and you do not touch its status. Annotating moves you on to the next card, like every other decision here; {{ key('prev') }} goes back.
{{ key('reject') }} reject, and what it is not
rejected means this card should not exist. It is not a way to park something: an annotated card is already refused by sync whatever its status, so a note alone holds it back. Rejecting to clear it from the draft list leaves a card Anki is still showing you while the repo says never sync it, and sync never deletes. Use no notes in the filter rail to review only what is actually waiting on you.
{{ key('resolve-note') }} resolve
Resolve the card's first annotation, or click resolve on any one of them. Resolving takes the address off the line. There is no reply and no done-flag, because a note still addressed still blocks sync, so answering one with a second annotation leaves two notes blocking the card. What a pass leaves behind instead is a `resolved:` line saying what was asked and what it did, which is a record rather than work: the card folds those away under settled, and that is where to look when an edit is not the one you remember asking for. Resolve a @claude note once the work is done, and a @me note once you have taken the decision it was holding open. The edit is to ## notes, which is outside content_hash, so resolving does not send an approved card back to draft. {{ key('undo') }} puts a resolved note back, which matters most for one you did not write: forge feedback turns a comment or flag from Anki into an annotation and erases it there as it takes it, so the line here is the only copy of it. To fix a typo, or say a request again that came back misunderstood, click edit beside it: the line is rewritten where it stands and stays open, which is the half of this that resolve cannot do without destroying the note first.

what a card is made of

the two gradings
Click either to cycle it. frequency is how often this turns up and derivation is what reconstructing it would take. Together they decide the order Anki introduces new cards in: most useful first, then easiest. A card missing either sorts to the back as unjudged, so the ones nobody graded are the ones you meet last. Neither un-approves the card. They sit outside content_hash on the same argument as requires: approving a card is not approving its position in the queue, and you learn that a result is common rather than core by meeting it months later. The cycle passes through unset, so a grade given by a mis-click comes off by carrying on clicking.
web
Whether whoever augments this card may look things up. Inherited from the unit it was written from, then the source, then the repo, where it is off. Click to cycle allowed / no / inherit. Also unhashed: it is a permission rather than a claim the card makes.
front, then given
The question, and the setting it is asked in. Conditions sit above the answer, here and in Anki. A constraint you only see after answering is a verdict rather than a condition.
back
The shortest complete answer.
used for
One line, usually absent: where this result actually turns up. Prose says what it means; this says where it appears.
proof · prose
A short derivation when it is load-bearing, and one sentence of intuition when it earns its place.
verify
Python that checks the identity numerically. Never reaches Anki. It is a check on whoever wrote the card, and it is how three errors in the source were caught.
