what this stage decides

Two questions, and only two. Is this worth a card at all, and roughly what would the card be about. That is the whole of triage.

Everything on screen serves those two — the crop, the sentence you marked, what you wrote beside it, the neighbours. You do not have to be able to transcribe a unit to triage it: a marked passage carries its own sentence, a boxed figure carries nothing and is still obviously worth a card or obviously not.

An answer that needs more than yes, and it is about X is a brief, not a decision to postpone — Q queues and records one in the same keystroke. Depth belongs to the card stage, where /extract-cards and /augment pull the page, the pages either side and the source's conventions, and iterate until it can be approved. Here it buys nothing and costs the throughput this stage exists for.

the whole machine

{% include "_fsm.html" %}

the unit

where it is
the section, page and — where the source numbers things — the number it printed. Whatever the source names it by is what makes this unit's id stable: an equation number for a book that has them, a Zotero annotation key for a document you marked up. Neither is a position, which is why adding one more mark renumbers nothing.
the picture
rendered from the PDF on demand, showing a margin of surrounding page with the unit's own box drawn on it — how much is crop_context, and a source may set its own. The box is what this unit claims to be. If the box holds two numbered equations, or the equation continues outside it, segmentation got it wrong — annotate rather than card it. p cycles the crop, the whole page, and the whole document scrolling.
the colours on it
what a reader marked, painted back on in the colours they used. The mark this unit came from is at full strength; everything marked nearby is faded, so a page with six highlights on it still says which one the card is about. A unit that came from a mark is cut to the full width of the page — a mark's box is the union of the lines a sentence happened to span, so its left and right edges are wherever that sentence started and stopped mid-column.
the marks underneath
what each one actually says — the sentence it covers, and anything written beside it. Whether a marked claim is worth a card on its own is mostly a question about its neighbours, and this is them. One marked its own unit you will meet again; the rest are only context for this decision. Two filters over that list, cutting across each other: the group headings fold a kind away, and the swatches above them switch a colour off. Both are yours alone — they change nothing on disk and they are not the rail's "marked as" filter, which decides which units you meet. Narrowing the queue to green claims while also hiding every purple term beside them would remove exactly the context this decision needs.
the maths beside it
the transcription, rendered. A hint, not content: where it disagrees with the picture, the picture wins. transcribed means something read the crop; not transcribed means run /transcribe, or read the crop yourself.
the grey text underneath
the prose immediately above the equation in the book — the sentence that introduces it. Context for deciding, never a transcription.

what each key does

q queue
This becomes a card. The greenlight: /extract-cards reads everything queued and writes a stub for it. Nothing reaches Anki without you approving the card afterwards, in the review view.
Q queue, and say what it is about
The same, plus the @claude brief, in one keystroke. This is the second half of what this stage decides and it only had a key by accident before — a separate n, on a unit that had usually scrolled past, which is why so many units reach /extract-cards carrying nothing but a picture. One sentence is enough: two cards, one per convention, this is about why the bound is tight, not the bound, merge with the one above. Symmetric with s/S: the bare key does not stop to ask, the shifted one records the sentence that makes the decision useful a month later.
s skip
Never offer this again. Sticky — it survives re-extraction. You are asked for a one-word reason, which is stored.
S skip, with a reason
The same, but asks for one word first. Plain s does not, because a skip is the commonest key in triage and stopping to type turned one keystroke into a dialogue.
u back to new
Undo a decision: the unit returns to the untriaged pool. This is the only way back from a skip.
a accept · d dismiss
Act on a suggestion, or throw it away. Suggestions come from forge classify or from /classify; both only ever propose. Pressing q or s yourself also clears the suggestion — you overruled it.
n / N the brief, and what you parked
n writes the @claude brief: the instruction /extract-cards reads before it writes anything — "two cards, one per layout convention", "the transcription is wrong, read the crop", "merge with the one above". It is the only instruction that pass gets, which is why it sits open in the panel rather than behind a fold. N parks an @me decision for yourself instead. Both show up in forge todo.
answering an @me
yes, no, or your own words. The answer is written back with the question — same as 2.4? — no — and unaddressed, so it is a record rather than new work. The × deletes without recording anything, which is right when the note was a reminder rather than a question.
c context size
How much of the document whoever writes this card is handed. The numbers are pages either side: 3 hands over seven pages, this one and three each way. Every size is on the chip; click one, or press c to walk them. Cycles through those rather than asking for a number, because the decision is "a bit more" or "all of it" and not a measurement. This is the moment you can tell — the theorem is here and its hypotheses are two pages back, and no per-source default knows that. The badge is always on screen and says whose number it is — source until this unit overrides it — because hiding it until it had already been changed meant never seeing what you were changing. Click it, or press c.
w web lookups
Whether whoever writes this card may look things up on the web. Off everywhere unless you grant it, and the default is the point: a card is supposed to say what this source says, hypotheses and notation included, and the web is full of cleaner statements of the general theorem. One of those substituted for the printed one looks like a better card right up until the thing you are actually studying turns on the condition the paper had and the internet did not. Grant it here, on a unit that cites a result the source never states — that is the moment you can see the difference. Three states, not two: allowed, no, and neither, which falls back to the source and then the repo. Clicking the one already in force clears the override. A card written from a granted unit inherits the grant, and forge context says which of the three answered.
p crop / page / document
Three ways to look at the same geometry. page is the whole page with the unit's own box drawn on it: the two ways segmentation fails are only visible against the surroundings, since a split equation shows its missing lines outside the box and two merged into one show two numbers inside it. document is every page, scrolling, landing on this one — for when the question is not about the region at all but about what the passage is saying, and the answer is three paragraphs up. Three named buttons in the corner of the picture do the same thing in one click each. Clicking the picture itself does nothing, deliberately: it used to cycle the view, which meant leaning in to read a highlight jumped you to the whole document.
z undo
Undo the last decision, however it was made — a state change, an accepted suggestion, a dismissed one. It restores what was actually there before, including the reason and any suggestion the action cleared, so a mis-pressed key costs one keystroke. Repeat to walk further back.
j / k
Next / previous. Queuing and skipping move you on to the next unit still awaiting a decision — but the one you just acted on stays in the list, so k always gets you back to it.
f filters · ? this guide
Both panels toggle. The guide cycles three sizes: counts, then everything, then hidden. Drag either edge to resize it. The left rail is what acts; this one only tells you what you are looking at.
g sources
Every source at once, with how far along each one is. Narrow it by tag or by where the material came from.

filters

new / queued / skipped / carded
The unit's state. new is untriaged, carded means a card exists for it.
transcribed
Only units whose tex_auto passed the KaTeX gate, so the maths is written out beside the crop instead of only pictured. The crop stays authoritative — if the two disagree, believe the crop and annotate the unit.
suggested
Only units something has proposed a decision for. A suggestion is never applied on its own — it waits. Most units have none, and that is a result rather than a gap: the classifier is not permitted to propose a skip for an equation the book gave a number, because numbering it is the document asserting it is a result. Those take a direct decision, and a/d do nothing on them.

what this stage decides

Whether the content is right. The unit stage already settled that this is worth a card and roughly what about; nothing here revisits that. What is open is the wording, the hypotheses, the grading and the order — everything that has to be true before it reaches Anki.

This is where depth belongs. /augment has the page, the pages either side and the source's conventions, and iterates until check passes; a card can be revised any number of times while it is a draft. Approving is the only irreversible-feeling step, and it is yours alone.

what each key does

a approve
This card is right. Stamps content_hash, and sync will push it to Anki. Editing it afterwards un-approves it — the hash stops matching and it returns here.
u back to draft
Un-approve a card you have changed your mind about. Editing one does this as a side effect — content_hash stops matching — but changing your mind is not an edit, and a token change in the editor would be a worse way to say so. Clears the hash, so nothing syncs it.
r reject
This card is wrong and is not worth fixing. It stays on disk; nothing syncs it.
e open in $EDITOR
Fix it yourself. The app re-reads from disk, and refuses to write over a file that changed underneath it.
z undo
Undo the last approve or reject. Approving stamps content_hash and rejecting drops it, so this restores the hash too — the card goes back to the state it was actually in, not merely to the same status. The card you just acted on also stays in the list, so k reaches it.
n / N annotate
n leaves an @claude note, a request for Claude; N parks an @me decision instead. Sync refuses any card with an open annotation of either kind, whatever its status, so the note alone holds the card back and you do not touch its status. Annotating moves you on to the next card, like every other decision here; k goes back.
r reject — and what it is not
rejected means this card should not exist. It is not a way to park something: an annotated card is already refused by sync whatever its status, so a note alone is enough to hold it back. Rejecting to clear it from the draft list leaves a card Anki is still showing you while the repo says never sync it — sync never deletes. Use no notes in the filter rail to review only what is actually waiting on you.
x resolve
Resolve the card's first annotation, or click resolve on any one of them. Resolving is deleting the line: there is no reply and no done-flag, because a note still in the file still blocks sync. Answering it with a second annotation therefore makes things worse, not better — now two notes block the card. Resolve a @claude note once the work is done; resolve a @me note once you have taken the decision it was holding open. The edit is to ## notes, which is outside content_hash, so resolving does not send an approved card back to draft.
j / k
Next / previous. Approving or rejecting moves you on to the next card still awaiting a decision; the one you just acted on stays in the list, so k reaches it.

what a card is made of

the two gradings
Click either to cycle it. frequency is how often this turns up, derivation is what reconstructing it would take, and together they decide the order Anki introduces new cards in — most useful first, then easiest. A card missing either sorts to the back as unjudged, so the ones nobody graded are the ones you meet last. Neither un-approves the card: they are outside content_hash on the same argument as requires — approving a card is not approving its position in the queue, and you learn that a result is common rather than core by meeting it, months later. The cycle passes through unset, so one given by a mis-click comes off by carrying on clicking.
web
Whether whoever augments this card may look things up. Inherited from the unit it was written from, then the source, then the repo — where it is off. Click to cycle allowed / no / inherit. Also unhashed: it is a permission, not a claim the card makes.
front, then given
The question, and the setting it is asked in. Conditions sit above the answer, here and in Anki: a constraint you only see after answering is a verdict, not a condition.
back
The shortest complete answer.
used for
One line, usually absent: where this result actually turns up. Distinct from prose — prose says what it means, this says where it appears.
proof · prose
A short derivation when it is load-bearing; one sentence of intuition when it earns its place.
verify
Python that checks the identity numerically. Never reaches Anki — it is a check on whoever wrote the card, and it is how three errors in the source were caught.
